# Chapter 4: Spatiotemporal Distribution and Trends

## 4.1 Spatial Distribution of Pollutants Across Physiographic Zones
The spatial distribution of atmospheric pollutants in Nepal exhibits a stark and statistically significant altitudinal gradient, largely dictated by the region's complex topography and proximity to major emission sources in the Indo-Gangetic Plain (IGP). 

### 4.1.1 Nitrogen Dioxide (NO₂)
Tropospheric NO₂ concentrations demonstrated the most pronounced spatial stratification. The annual mean NO₂ density was highest in the low-lying **Terai** region ($26.10 \pm 5.18 \times 10^{14} \text{ molecules/cm}^2$), reflecting the intense agricultural activity, high population density, and vehicular emissions characteristic of the southern border. Concentrations decreased monotonically with elevation, passing through the **Siwalik** ($20.79 \pm 5.13 \times 10^{14}$), the **Middle Mountains** ($15.19 \pm 2.97 \times 10^{14}$), and the **High Mountains** ($10.00 \pm 1.89 \times 10^{14}$). The lowest concentrations were recorded in the pristine **High Himal** region ($8.24 \pm 1.61 \times 10^{14}$), which functions effectively as a clean background site for the Indian subcontinent.

The variability (Coefficient of Variation, CV) was highest in the Siwalik (24.67%), suggesting that this transitional foothill zone is highly susceptible to episodic pollution events, such as transboundary advection from the IGP and seasonal forest fires.

## 4.2 Annual and Seasonal Variations
The temporal dynamics of atmospheric pollution in Nepal are overwhelmingly governed by the South Asian monsoon system, which bifurcates the year into distinct phases of accumulation and wet deposition.

### 4.2.1 Seasonal Climatology
Across all physiographic zones, the **Pre-monsoon** (March-May) and **Winter** (December-February) seasons exhibited the highest NO₂ concentrations. For instance, in the Middle Mountains (which encompasses the Kathmandu Valley), NO₂ peaked during the Pre-monsoon at $17.93 \times 10^{14} \text{ molecules/cm}^2$, followed closely by Winter at $13.54 \times 10^{14} \text{ molecules/cm}^2$. 

Conversely, the **Monsoon** season (June-September) acts as an intense atmospheric sink. Heavy precipitation and dominant southerly winds facilitate the wet deposition of pollutants and atmospheric cleansing. During this period, NO₂ concentrations in the Siwalik plummeted to $19.78 \times 10^{14}$, the lowest seasonal average for the lower elevation zones. The **Post-monsoon** (October-November) period marks the cessation of rain and the onset of cyclical crop residue burning in the Terai, initiating the annual accumulation cycle.

## 4.3 Pixel-wise Trend Analysis Results (2019–2026)
To ascertain whether these spatial and seasonal patterns are shifting over time, a robust Mann-Kendall (MK) trend analysis was applied to the 8-year dataset.

### 4.3.1 Annual Trends
At the annual scale, NO₂ did not exhibit a statistically significant monotonic trend ($p > 0.05$) in any of the five physiographic zones. While the Sen's slopes were slightly positive (e.g., $+0.033 \times 10^{14}$ per month in the Siwalik), the extreme interannual variability—heavily influenced by the anomalously low emissions during the 2020 COVID-19 lockdowns and the severe 2021 wildfire season—obscured long-term directional shifts at the annual aggregate level.

### 4.3.2 Seasonal Shifts and Winter Deterioration
When disaggregated by season, a critical and concerning trend emerged. **Winter NO₂ concentrations are exhibiting a statistically significant increasing trend** across multiple mid-to-high elevation zones:
- **Siwalik:** Significant increase ($p = 0.020$, Slope = $+0.194$)
- **Middle Mountains:** Significant increase ($p = 0.023$, Slope = $+0.161$)
- **High Mountains:** Significant increase ($p = 0.015$, Slope = $+0.140$)

This Winter deterioration is likely driven by a combination of factors: increased localized wintertime fossil fuel combustion for heating, the intensification of winter temperature inversions trapping pollutants in valleys, and potentially increased transport of boundary layer pollution from the IGP driven by synoptic winter westerlies. Notably, the High Himal region remains unaffected ($p = 0.139$), maintaining its pristine status even during winter months.

## 4.4 Discussion on Altitudinal Pollution Gradients
The robust KDE Ridge Plots (Joyplots) generated from the Sentinel-5P dataset visually confirm the "pooling" effect of the Himalayas. The massive Himalayan massif acts as a physical barrier, trapping advected IGP pollution in the Terai and Siwalik regions. As elevation increases, the atmospheric column thins and distance from primary emission sources increases, leading to the sharply compressed distributions observed in the High Mountains and High Himal. 

However, the significant upward trend of Winter NO₂ in the Middle and High Mountains suggests that this topographic isolation is being increasingly compromised during the colder months, a finding with profound implications for the highly vulnerable cryosphere and glacier melt dynamics in the Himalayas.
